## Grading severity
Severe AS is any of: peak aortic velocity ≥4.0 m/s, mean gradient ≥40 mmHg, or
valve area ≤1.0 cm² (indexed ≤0.6 cm²/m²). Very severe is Vmax ≥5.0 m/s.

Velocity and gradient are flow-dependent, and that is the trap: both fall when
stroke volume falls, so a small valve can produce a low gradient and read as
moderate. Area is the flow-independent measure, which is why the three
criteria disagree in exactly the patients who matter most.

When echo is equivocal, aortic valve calcium score on non-contrast CT is
flow-independent and settles it: severe AS is likely at ≥2000 AU in men and
≥1200 AU in women, and very likely at ≥3000 and ≥1600. Useful precisely
because it does not care what the stroke volume is doing.

Dimensionless index (LVOT VTI ÷ aortic VTI) is the other flow-independent
escape: ≤0.25 indicates severe stenosis, and because it is a ratio it cancels
out the LVOT diameter measurement that most often corrupts a calculated valve
area. Squaring an LVOT radius means any error in it is squared too, which is
why a mis-measured LVOT is the commonest reason a valve area looks severe when
it is not.

## Low-gradient severe AS
Two distinct entities, and they are managed differently.

**Classical low-flow, low-gradient** — valve area ≤1.0 cm², mean gradient
<40 mmHg, LVEF <50%. Do dobutamine stress echo. If stroke volume rises and the
gradient climbs above 40 mmHg while the area stays ≤1.0, the stenosis is truly
severe. If the area opens up instead, it was pseudo-severe: a weak ventricle
failing to open a moderately diseased valve, and replacing it will not help.
Absence of contractile reserve (stroke volume rising <20%) predicts high
operative mortality but does not by itself exclude benefit.

**Paradoxical low-flow, low-gradient** — the same low gradient with a
*preserved* EF, driven by a small, stiff, concentrically remodelled ventricle
that ejects a normal fraction of a small volume. Stroke volume index is
<35 mL/m². It is commonest in elderly women with hypertension and marked
concentric hypertrophy, and it carries a worse prognosis than its numbers
suggest.

Before accepting it, confirm the measurements are not simply wrong — an
underestimated LVOT diameter or a poorly aligned Doppler beam produces the
same picture — and check the patient is not merely hypertensive at the time of
the study, since high afterload depresses flow and mimics it.

## Examination
The murmur that is *late-peaking*, with a soft or absent A2 and a single S2,
is the severe one — intensity is not the marker, and a loud murmur can soften
as the ventricle fails and stroke volume drops. A delayed, low-amplitude
carotid upstroke (pulsus parvus et tardus) is specific but blunted by stiff
arteries in the elderly, so its absence proves nothing in an 80-year-old.

Gallavardin phenomenon: the high-frequency component of the murmur radiates to
the apex and mimics mitral regurgitation. It is the same murmur reaching a
different place, not a second lesion — the giveaway is that it stays
mid-systolic and ejection-quality rather than becoming holosystolic.

Handgrip raises afterload and *softens* the AS murmur. Valsalva reduces
preload and also softens it, while the murmur of hypertrophic obstruction gets
*louder* — less preload means a smaller cavity and more obstruction. That
opposite response is the bedside discriminator, and it is the one boards ask
about.

The classic triad is angina, syncope and heart failure, and each carries its
own prognosis untreated: roughly 5, 3 and 2 years respectively. Angina occurs
with normal coronaries because a hypertrophied ventricle outgrows its supply
and the subendocardium is perfused against a high cavity pressure.

## When to replace the valve
Class 1: symptomatic severe AS, at any gradient, once true severity is
established. Class 1: asymptomatic severe AS with LVEF <50% not attributable
to another cause. Class 1: severe AS in a patient already undergoing cardiac
surgery for another indication.

Class 2a: asymptomatic *very* severe AS (Vmax ≥5 m/s) at low surgical risk;
asymptomatic severe AS with a fall in exercise tolerance or a drop in blood
pressure on exercise testing; and asymptomatic severe AS with a rapidly rising
velocity or a markedly elevated natriuretic peptide.

The decision between SAVR and transcatheter replacement turns on age, surgical
risk, anatomy and expected valve durability — not on the severity grade, which
only decides *whether* to intervene. In broad terms, younger patients at low
risk with long life expectancy favour surgical replacement for durability;
older or higher-risk patients favour transcatheter.

Exercise testing is contraindicated once a patient with severe AS is
symptomatic — it is a tool for unmasking symptoms in someone who claims to
have none, not for grading a patient who has already declared themselves.
Medical therapy does not alter the natural history: there is no drug that
slows the valve. Statins were tested and failed.
