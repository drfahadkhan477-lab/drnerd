## The four pillars
Four drug classes independently reduce mortality in HFrEF, and the modern
approach is to start all four early at low dose rather than titrating one to
target before beginning the next. Sequential titration takes months; the
benefit of each class appears within weeks.

**Renin-angiotensin inhibition** — an ARNI (sacubitril/valsartan) is preferred
over an ACE inhibitor, on PARADIGM-HF. **Beta-blockade** — only carvedilol,
metoprolol succinate and bisoprolol have mortality evidence, and they are not
interchangeable with other beta-blockers. **Mineralocorticoid receptor
antagonism** — spironolactone (RALES) or eplerenone (EMPHASIS-HF).
**SGLT2 inhibition** — dapagliflozin (DAPA-HF) or empagliflozin
(EMPEROR-Reduced).

The SGLT2 benefit is independent of diabetes. These are heart-failure drugs
that happen to lower glucose, not glucose drugs that happen to help — which is
why a non-diabetic patient with HFrEF still gets one.

Expect and tolerate a modest early fall in blood pressure and a small rise in
creatinine. Stopping GDMT for an asymptomatic 20–30% creatinine rise is a
common and costly error; the drugs that transiently raise creatinine are the
ones protecting the kidney long term.

## Starting an ARNI safely
Sacubitril/valsartan must not be given within 36 hours of an ACE inhibitor.
Both raise bradykinin, and overlapping them stacks angioedema risk — the
washout is the single most important practical rule about the drug.

A prior history of angioedema on any ACE inhibitor is an absolute
contraindication to an ARNI. So is pregnancy, as with every
renin-angiotensin-acting agent.

There is no washout needed coming from an ARB, since ARBs do not act on
bradykinin.

PARADIGM-HF compared sacubitril/valsartan against enalapril in ambulatory
patients and was stopped early for benefit: fewer cardiovascular deaths and
heart-failure hospitalisations. PIONEER-HF then showed it could be started in
hospital after stabilisation of an acute episode, which is why initiation no
longer waits for an outpatient visit.

Monitor potassium and renal function. Neprilysin inhibition raises natriuretic
peptides by design, so BNP is no longer interpretable as a marker of
decompensation on this drug — NT-proBNP is not a neprilysin substrate and
remains usable.

## When drugs are not enough
**Ivabradine** slows the sinus node through the funny current and is for
patients in sinus rhythm with a heart rate ≥70 despite a maximally tolerated
beta-blocker, EF ≤35%, and symptoms. It does nothing in atrial fibrillation,
because it acts only on the sinus node — a distinction worth holding onto.
SHIFT is the trial.

**Hydralazine with isosorbide dinitrate** reduced mortality in
self-identified Black patients already on standard therapy (A-HeFT), and is
also the fallback when renin-angiotensin blockade cannot be used at all
because of renal dysfunction or hyperkalaemia.

**Vericiguat**, a soluble guanylate cyclase stimulator, reduced the composite
of cardiovascular death and heart-failure hospitalisation after a recent
worsening event (VICTORIA). It occupies a narrow niche: the patient who has
just decompensated despite good therapy.

**Digoxin** reduces hospitalisation without reducing mortality. It is a
symptom and admission drug, and its narrow therapeutic window means the target
level is low.

## Devices
**ICD** for primary prevention: EF ≤35%, NYHA class II–III, on optimal medical
therapy for at least three months, with a reasonable expectation of survival
beyond a year. The three-month wait is not bureaucratic — ejection fraction
improves enough on GDMT in a substantial minority that the indication
disappears, and implanting early means implanting people who no longer
qualify. After myocardial infarction the wait is 40 days.

**CRT** is Class 1 with EF ≤35%, sinus rhythm, left bundle branch block and
QRS ≥150 ms, in NYHA II–IV on GDMT. Benefit falls as you move away from that
description: non-LBBB morphology and QRS 120–149 ms are weaker indications,
and CRT in a narrow QRS is harmful.

The reason is mechanistic. CRT resynchronises a ventricle made dyssynchronous
by conduction delay. Without delay there is nothing to resynchronise, and
pacing simply introduces the dyssynchrony it was meant to fix — which is why
narrow-QRS CRT trials showed harm rather than merely no benefit.

Anticipated high burden of right ventricular pacing is itself an indication to
consider a resynchronising or conduction-system device rather than a standard
pacemaker, for the same reason: RV apical pacing creates the very conduction
delay CRT exists to correct.
