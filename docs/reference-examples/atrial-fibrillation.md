## Deciding on anticoagulation
CHA2DS2-VASc scores congestive heart failure, hypertension, age ≥75 (2
points), diabetes, prior stroke or TIA (2 points), vascular disease, age
65–74, and female sex category. Anticoagulate at ≥2 in men and ≥3 in women
(Class 1); at 1 in men or 2 in women it is reasonable (Class 2a).

Female sex is a risk *modifier*, not an independent risk factor — it amplifies
the others rather than standing alone, which is why a woman with no other
factors scores 1 and is not anticoagulated.

The decision does not depend on whether the AF is paroxysmal, persistent or
permanent, and it does not depend on rhythm control succeeding. A patient in
sinus rhythm after ablation with a score of 3 still needs anticoagulation;
ablation treats symptoms, not stroke risk.

Bleeding scores such as HAS-BLED identify *modifiable* risk to correct —
uncontrolled hypertension, labile INR, alcohol, concomitant NSAIDs — and
should not be used to withhold anticoagulation. A high bleeding score in a
patient with a high stroke score usually argues for fixing the bleeding risk,
not for leaving the stroke risk untreated.

## Choosing the anticoagulant
A direct oral anticoagulant is preferred over warfarin in eligible patients
(Class 1). The two absolute exceptions are a mechanical prosthetic valve and
moderate-to-severe rheumatic mitral stenosis: both require warfarin, because
the DOACs were either untested or actively harmful there — dabigatran
increased both thromboembolism and bleeding after mechanical valve
replacement in RE-ALIGN.

"Valvular AF" as a phrase causes more confusion than it resolves. It does not
mean any valve disease. It means those two situations. A patient with severe
aortic stenosis, mitral regurgitation, or a bioprosthetic valve may have a
DOAC.

Dose reduction criteria are drug-specific and are a common source of
under-dosing. Apixaban is reduced to 2.5 mg twice daily only when two of
three are present: age ≥80, weight ≤60 kg, creatinine ≥1.5 mg/dL. Reducing on
one criterion alone is a real-world error that costs efficacy without buying
safety.

Left atrial appendage occlusion is an option when there is a genuine
long-term contraindication to anticoagulation, not merely a preference to
avoid it.

## Rate versus rhythm control
AFFIRM found no mortality advantage to rhythm control, and that result shaped
practice for two decades. EAST-AFNET 4 qualified it: rhythm control started
*within a year of diagnosis* reduced cardiovascular death, stroke and
hospitalisation. Timing is the variable those trials disagree about, so early
diagnosis argues for rhythm control while long-standing AF does not.

For rate control, RACE II showed lenient control (resting heart rate <110) was
non-inferior to strict control in patients who were asymptomatic with
preserved ventricular function. In symptomatic patients, or where a
tachycardia-mediated cardiomyopathy is suspected, aim lower.

Beta-blockers and non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers are first line;
the latter are contraindicated in HFrEF because of their negative inotropy.
Digoxin is an adjunct, not a first choice, and is most useful in sedentary
patients or as an add-on — it controls rate at rest far better than on
exertion, because its effect is vagally mediated.

Catheter ablation is Class 1 for symptomatic paroxysmal AF refractory to or
intolerant of antiarrhythmic drugs, and is reasonable as first-line therapy in
selected younger patients with few comorbidities. In HFrEF, CASTLE-AF showed
ablation reduced death and heart-failure hospitalisation.

## Cardioverting safely
AF of more than 48 hours, or of unknown duration, requires either three weeks
of therapeutic anticoagulation beforehand or a transoesophageal echo excluding
left atrial thrombus. Either way, anticoagulation continues for at least four
weeks afterwards.

The four weeks are not optional and are the step most often dropped. Atrial
mechanical function recovers more slowly than electrical function — atrial
stunning — so the highest-risk window for thromboembolism is *after* sinus
rhythm is restored, not before. A patient who looks and feels fixed is at
their most vulnerable.

Beyond four weeks, whether to continue is decided by CHA2DS2-VASc, not by the
apparent success of the cardioversion.

Haemodynamic instability overrides all of this: cardiovert immediately and
anticoagulate as soon as it is safe. For AF clearly under 48 hours the
pre-treatment window can be compressed, though anticoagulation at the time of
cardioversion is still given.

Dronedarone is contraindicated in decompensated heart failure and in permanent
AF — ANDROMEDA and PALLAS both stopped early for excess mortality. That is a
pair of trials worth remembering by name, because the drug is otherwise
superficially attractive.
